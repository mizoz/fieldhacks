SlopeShift · Classroom kit · Ages 14+

Build a wind logger, then forecast the wind

SlopeShift is a kit for high-school science and engineering classes. Students build a wind vane and anemometer that log every minute for about 20 days. Then they find when the wind turned each morning and evening, and learn to forecast it from the sun.

Ask about classroom quotes Try the Analyzer

In development. The firmware is written, tested on a PC and compiles for the board. The analysis tools work on made-up demo data. No unit has been built and run outdoors yet, so we're not quoting accuracy figures. If you'd like to pilot a kit with a class, get in touch.

What students do

  1. Build it. Solder a reed-switch vane and resistor ladder, fit the cups and magnet, and seal the electronics in a PVC tube. Two or three class periods.
  2. Log real wind. Set the clock, calibrate the cups, and leave it outside for one to two weeks. It records direction and speed every minute.
  3. Find the switch. Load the log in the Analyzer. On calm, clear days, local winds turn around each morning and evening. Students see when, and on which days the weather overrode it.
  4. Forecast it. Describe the site (slope, the direction it faces, any ridges) and the Analyzer learns how the switch follows the sun. Students predict the switch for dates they haven't measured, then test the predictions against new data.

Schoolyards are often flat, and flat ground gets weak slope winds. Near a lake or the sea, students can log the lake or sea breeze instead: the Analyzer has a shore setting for it. For a quick demo in class, the logger's fast mode prints a reading every 2 seconds and flags the switch when you turn a fan or hairdryer around.

What's in the box

  • Electronics: Pro Mini 3.3 V board with the firmware already loaded, DS3231 clock with coin cell, 24LC256 memory chip and socket, perfboard and headers.
  • Sensors: 9 glass reed switches plus spares, 1% ladder resistors, a capacitor, and small neodymium magnets.
  • Mechanical: bearings, bolts, PVC tube and caps, crossbar, cable, a 2×AA holder with switch, and fittings.
  • Printed parts: vane, hub, reed ring, cup rotor, clamps and mast bracket.
  • Cable: a 3.3 V USB-serial adapter for setting it up and reading the log. Classroom packs share three.

You also need: 2 AA batteries, a stake or pole, a soldering iron and solder, wire cutters, a compass, and a computer with a web browser.

What students learn

The teacher guide has five lessons, each with a student worksheet and an answer key:

  1. Why slopes make wind. Heating, cooling and air density; why the wind turns around twice a day.
  2. Sensors. Reed switches, the resistor ladder, and the maths that turns one analog reading into 16 directions.
  3. Build and deploy. Assembly, calibration, and choosing a good spot.
  4. Data. Why you can't average directions like ordinary numbers, and how to find the switch in a noisy log.
  5. Model and predict. The sun's path and sun on a slope; make predictions, then test them against new data.

It fits Earth science, physics and engineering design units. We're still checking it against specific standards and provincial curricula.

The Analyzer

The SlopeShift Analyzer runs in a web browser, with nothing to install and no student accounts. The log stays on the student's computer. It shows each day's switch, wind by time of day, and a forecast for any dates you choose. You can try it now with two weeks of demo data (made up, for practice).

How it compares

Classroom weather and wind kits we found cost roughly $220 to $250. Some are one calibrated sensor for a whole class, and some are paper-plate builds that don't record anything. SlopeShift gives each group its own logger, and the data work keeps going after the build. A calibrated commercial sensor is the better instrument, and SlopeShift's readings depend on how carefully students build and calibrate it. We couldn't find a classroom kit where students forecast their own site's wind from the sun.
